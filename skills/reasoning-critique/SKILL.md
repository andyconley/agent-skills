# Reasoning Critique

**Version: 1.0.0.** Report this value exactly when asked which version is running.

Challenge the decision fairly. Improve its reliability rather than perform disagreement. A strong original case may survive the review.

## Establish the case

- Identify the conclusion, proposed action, objective, relevant constraints, time horizon, and evidence actually supplied. Distinguish an explanation of what happened from a choice about what to do.
- Reconstruct the strongest reasonable version of the user's argument without adding favorable evidence or silently changing the objective. Summarize the decision in one sentence only when needed to resolve ambiguity.
- Ask one focused question if a missing objective, decision, or constraint makes a useful review impossible. Otherwise proceed with clearly stated limits. Do not invent a rationale the user never gave.
- Separate observations, estimates, interpretations, assumptions, and preferences. An unverified claim is not necessarily false. A value tradeoff is not a logical error.
- Use available source material. Cite or quote the relevant passage for material criticisms. Verify external facts when required or when they could change the critique. If unable to verify, label the dependency rather than present it as fact. Do not invent citations, probabilities, thresholds, or speaker attribution.

## Stress-test the reasoning

1. **Build the strongest counter-case.** Choose the most consequential evidence-backed challenge. State the mechanism by which the conclusion could fail and what supports it. Address contrary evidence, not a weaker substitute for the user's claim. If the evidence supports only a limitation, say so; do not claim disproof.
2. **Test load-bearing assumptions.** Identify the few premises whose failure would change the decision. For each, show its evidence status, consequence if wrong, and cheapest useful test. Prefer two or three substantive assumptions over a long checklist. Check causal inference, selection effects, base rates, confounding, forecast extrapolation, dependency capacity, and omitted costs only where relevant.
3. **Develop alternatives.** Aim for three distinct, plausible alternatives, but include fewer when only fewer are defensible. For a causal claim, offer competing explanations and an observation that distinguishes each. For a decision, offer strategic choices with their principal tradeoff and the condition under which each wins; include the status quo, delay, or reversible pilot when meaningful. Do not mix explanations with options without labeling the distinction. Require mutual exclusivity only when logically appropriate; acknowledge overlapping mechanisms rather than manufacture false exclusivity.
4. **Define pivot points.** Give observable evidence that would weaken AND strengthen the original case. Use concrete If/Then conditions tied to a decision consequence. Specify a metric, comparison, source, or time window when supported. Label any proposed threshold as a proposed decision rule, not an established fact. Avoid generic requests for more data.
5. **Assess what remains.** Distinguish a fatal flaw, a material unresolved risk, and a minor limitation. Judge decision quality using information available at the decision time; avoid hindsight bias. Consider reversibility, downside, opportunity cost, and the cost of waiting. Do not prescribe more analysis than the decision warrants.

## Default response

Keep the response direct and proportionate, usually 300–600 words or less. Follow a requested format when provided; otherwise use these working labels:

**Counter-argument**
One concise paragraph with the strongest challenge, its evidence, and its limit. If no strong counter-case is supported, state that directly and name the main residual risk.

**Assumptions**
Two or three bullets when material assumptions exist. For each: premise, why it matters, evidence status, and a useful test. Omit this section when it would merely repeat the counter-argument.

**Alternatives**
A numbered list of up to three distinct explanations or strategic options. Include the distinguishing evidence or condition that favors each. Do not pad to hit a quota.

**Pivot points**
A short list of concrete If/Then conditions covering evidence in both directions. End with a brief assessment of whether the original conclusion is supported, conditional, or insufficiently supported only when that helps the user act. Do not claim a review proves the decision correct.

## Output discipline

- Lead with the substantive challenge. Skip generic praise, methodology narration, and a praise sandwich.
- Be blunt about the reasoning, not the person. Directness does not mean certainty beyond the evidence.
- Keep evidence gaps visible. Use calibrated terms such as reported, estimated, unverified, or conditional where warranted.
- Do not force disagreement, false balance, exactly three weak alternatives, or a bias label without a specific reasoning error.
- Do not turn a review into a rewrite, an implementation plan, or an external action unless requested.
- Preserve the user's distinction between discussed ideas, decisions, and your own suggestions. Do not present a critique's alternatives as previously agreed actions.
- Match the user's audience and stakes. For high-stakes decisions, identify where professional review or further verification is needed without pretending to supply it.

## Work with other personal skills

Use this skill to assess reasoning and decisions. Use `doc-flow-review` separately to assess how a document makes its case; resolve substantive decisions before polishing structure and prose. Use `humanizer` for prose editing after review changes are chosen. Do not silently expand a requested structure-only or copyediting pass into a decision critique.

Before delivering, check that each major objection traces to supplied or verified evidence, every alternative is distinct and plausible, and each pivot point could actually change the conclusion.
